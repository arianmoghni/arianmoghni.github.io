Galactic Rain: Cool Gas Inflows in Red Geyser Galaxies

MaNGA · Na I D Spectroscopy · AGN Feedback · Galaxy Quenching

140 Red geyser galaxies from SDSS-IV MaNGA
~70% Of cool neutral gas is inflowing, not outflowing
7× Larger inflow reservoirs in radio-detected systems
2.7× Larger reservoirs in interacting vs. isolated red geysers

One of the central puzzles in modern astronomy is understanding why some galaxies stop forming stars and, crucially, how they stay quiet for billions of years — even when they still contain gas that could, in principle, fuel new stars. Red geysers are a rare class of massive quiescent galaxies (log[M/M☉] ≈ 10.5) that exhibit large-scale but weak, bi-symmetric ionized gas outflows — interpreted as signatures of ongoing, low-level active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. But what fuels the AGN in the first place?

In this study, I used spatially-resolved spectra from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey-IV Mapping Nearby Galaxies at APO (MaNGA) survey to map the kinematics of cool (T ∼ 100–1000 K) neutral gas traced by the Na I D absorption doublet across 140 red geyser galaxies. By fitting double-Gaussian profiles to the doublet, I measured spatially resolved velocities and velocity dispersions — revealing whether the gas is flowing in or out.

"These galaxy-scale winds are thought to be signatures of supermassive black hole activity in the center. The puzzle is how these black holes get their fuel."

— Arian Moghni, UCSC press release, January 2026

Contrary to the outflowing picture commonly seen in star-forming galaxies, roughly 70% of the cool gas in red geysers shows signatures of inflow, with a median velocity of ~47 km/s — about 10% of the expected free-fall speed. This slow, organized infall points to a continuous drizzle of cool clouds raining down toward the galactic center, where they can feed the supermassive black hole.

A particularly striking result involves the role of the galaxy environment. Red geysers that show evidence of interactions with nearby companions, or signatures of past minor mergers, host inflowing gas reservoirs roughly 2.7 times larger than isolated systems. Meanwhile, the ~30% of red geysers detected in radio — a sign of active central black holes — have inflow reservoirs nearly 7 times larger than their radio-quiet counterparts.

Together, these findings support a self-regulating cycle: galaxy interactions deliver fresh cool gas, which rains inward and feeds the AGN; the AGN heats and stirs the surrounding gas; star formation remains suppressed; and the galaxy stays "red and dead." Rather than a one-time shutoff, quiescence appears to be an active, continuously maintained state.

Previous Research · Exoplanetary Science

Modeling Exoplanet Atmospheres

Atmospheric Escape · Photoionization · Hydrodynamics

Working with Prof. Ruth Murray-Clay at UC Santa Cruz, I investigated the physics of exoplanetary atmospheres — in particular the conditions under which atmospheric escape occurs and how upper-atmospheric structure responds to irradiation from the host star.

Close-in exoplanets receive intense high-energy (X-ray and EUV) radiation that can drive hydrodynamic escape of their envelopes, sculpting the observed population of planets. I developed and applied models to characterize these photoionization-driven outflows, examining how atmospheric composition and escape rates depend on stellar flux, planetary gravity, and the properties of the upper atmosphere.

This work connects to the broader question of how planet populations evolve: understanding which planets retain their atmospheres over gigayear timescales has direct implications for planetary habitability and the demographics revealed by missions like Kepler and TESS.

RR Lyrae Variable Stars

Pulsating Stars · Distance Indicators · Stellar Populations

RR Lyrae stars are ancient, pulsating horizontal-branch stars that serve as some of the most reliable standard candles in the cosmos. Because their periods and luminosities are tightly linked, they allow astronomers to measure distances to globular clusters, dwarf galaxies, and the halo of the Milky Way with high precision.
