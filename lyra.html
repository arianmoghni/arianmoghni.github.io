Galactic Rain: Cool Gas Inflows in Red Geyser Galaxies

Galaxy Evolution AGN Feedback Galaxy Quenching Spatially Resolved IFU Data
140 Red geyser galaxies
~70% Of cool neutral gas is inflowing
7× Larger inflow reservoirs in radio-detected systems
2.7× Larger reservoirs in interacting vs. isolated red geysers

One of the central puzzles in modern astronomy is understanding why some galaxies stop forming stars and, crucially, how they stay quiet for billions of years — even when they still contain gas that could, in principle, fuel new stars. Red geysers are a rare class of massive quiescent galaxies (log[M/M☉] ≈ 10.5) that exhibit large-scale but weak, bi-symmetric ionized gas outflows — interpreted as signatures of ongoing, low-level active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback. But what fuels the AGN in the first place?

I led an investigation into the mechanisms of quiescence by studying the cool and warm interstellar gas — traced by Na I D and Ca II absorption — in 140 red geyser galaxies at z ∼ 0.04. Using spatially resolved spectra from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey-IV Mapping Nearby Galaxies at APO (MaNGA) survey, I fitted double-Gaussian profiles to the Na I D doublet across each galaxy to measure spatially resolved velocities and velocity dispersions — revealing whether the gas is flowing in or out.

"These galaxy-scale winds are thought to be signatures of supermassive black hole activity in the center. The puzzle is how these black holes get their fuel."

— Arian Moghni, UCSC press release, January 2026

Contrary to the outflowing picture commonly seen in star-forming galaxies, roughly 70% of the cool gas in red geysers shows signatures of inflow, with a median velocity of ~47 km/s — about 10% of the expected free-fall speed. This slow, organized infall points to a continuous drizzle of cool clouds raining down toward the galactic center, where they can feed the supermassive black hole.

A particularly striking result involves the role of the galaxy environment. Red geysers that show evidence of interactions with nearby companions, or signatures of past minor mergers, host inflowing gas reservoirs roughly 2.7 times larger than isolated systems. Meanwhile, the ~30% of red geysers detected in radio — a sign of active central black holes — have inflow reservoirs nearly 7 times larger than their radio-quiet counterparts.

Together, these findings support a self-regulating cycle: galaxy interactions deliver fresh cool gas, which rains inward and feeds the AGN; the AGN heats and stirs the surrounding gas; star formation remains suppressed; and the galaxy stays "red and dead." Rather than a one-time shutoff, quiescence appears to be an active, continuously maintained state. This work resulted in two first-author papers (one in preparation) and a Best Undergraduate Research Poster award at the annual APS regional meeting. I was also 1 of 32 researchers — from over 2,900 attendees — invited to present at the 247th AAS Press Conference in January 2026.

Quantifying Confidence in Spectral Features

HST & JWST Spectroscopy Statistical Significance Gaussian Fitting Peer Mentorship

In this project I served as peer mentor to a team of four first-year astrophysics undergraduates, guiding them through the process of applying rigorous statistical tools to real observational data from the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

A persistent challenge in observational spectroscopy is determining whether a spectral feature — an emission or absorption line — is genuinely present in the data or is merely a noise fluctuation. I developed and implemented Gaussian fitting routines and χ2 tests to assess the statistical significance of spectral features in noisy HST and JWST spectra, and taught the team to apply these methods to their own datasets. The project gave the students hands-on experience with the full pipeline from raw spectra to quantitative confidence statements — skills directly transferable to any spectroscopic analysis.

Previous Research · January 2024 – June 2024 · UC Santa Cruz

Modelling the Torus of Hot Jupiters: Where To Look For Them?

Hot Jupiter Exoplanets Atmospheric Escape Spectral Simulation Order-of-Magnitude Calculations

Hot Jupiters — gas giant exoplanets on very close-in orbits — are subjected to intense irradiation from their host stars, driving hydrodynamic atmospheric escape that may form a diffuse plasma torus along the planet's orbital path. Detecting this torus would provide a direct window into atmospheric mass-loss rates and the long-term fate of close-in planets.

I built a forward model to predict the detectability of this proposed torus as a function of system properties: stellar mass and radius, gas temperature, orbital distance, and ionizing flux. The model simulates the expected observed spectra — including the resulting absorption lines — and returns the probability that a torus would be detectable for any given set of parameters. In practice, the model serves as a targeting tool: it can rank candidate Hot Jupiter systems by the likelihood of a torus detection, focusing future telescope time on the most promising targets and maximizing the chances of a discovery. Findings were presented at the UC Santa Cruz ASTR 9 Research Symposium.

Previous Research · June 2023 – August 2023 · UC Santa Cruz

Dependence of the Periodogram Peak Width of Variable Stars on Observational and Light Curve Parameters

Photometry Variable Stars Lomb–Scargle Periodogram

RR Lyrae are low-mass horizontal branch stars that undergo regular pulsations, giving them a characteristic light curve shape and a tight, shallow period–luminosity relation in the i-filter. These properties make them excellent standard candles and powerful tracers of Galactic structure and merger history. Accurate period determination is central to exploiting them — but how precisely can we pin down a period, and what controls that precision?

Working with Prof. Raja GuhaThakurta at UC Santa Cruz through the Science Internship Program (SIP) — awarded to the top 9% of applicants — I searched for new RR Lyrae stars in the Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope Legacy Survey (CFHTLS) using both the Lomb–Scargle periodogram and the Phase Distance Correlation method. I also tested a new heuristic formula that predicts the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the peak in the Generalized Lomb–Scargle (GLS) periodogram of a periodic variable. The formula relates the peak width to five parameters: the star's period, the amplitude and slope of its light curve, the photometric precision of the measurements, the number of measurements, and the time baseline over which they were taken.

How finely must you search for a period? The answer depends on how you observed — and on the shape of the light curve itself. A validated formula now provides a principled answer for any time-domain survey.

To test the formula empirically, we used time-series photometry in six filters (u, g, r, i1, i2, z) from the CFHTLS Deep Fields, imaging four 1 deg² fields with the CFHT 3.6-m telescope and MegaCam over nearly a decade. These empirical tests validate the heuristic formula across a wide range of parameter space. The formula tells observers how finely they need to sample period space when searching for RR Lyrae, and is broadly applicable to periodic variables in any time-domain survey — including the forthcoming Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST).
